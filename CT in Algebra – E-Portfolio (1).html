Overview & Rationale

This e-portfolio analyses four basic-algebra activities (two unplugged, two plugged-in) through the six lenses of computational thinking (CT): decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithms, logical reasoning and evaluation. Unplugged tasks build concrete understanding without a screen; plugged-in tasks add speed, feedback and visualisation. Together they form a balanced learning pathway: concrete → pictorial → symbolic → digital.

Critical Analysis: Unplugged vs Plugged-in

CriterionUnpluggedPlugged-in
StrengthsConcrete, tactile, collaborative; no device needed; reveals misconceptions.Instant feedback, unlimited practice, dynamic visuals, self-paced.
LimitationsSlow to repeat; hard to scale; limited accuracy for large numbers.Risk of "button-clicking" without understanding; needs devices and internet.
EffectivenessHigh for building conceptual meaning of equality and variables.High for fluency, exploring many cases and linking representations.
ImprovementsAdd recording sheets and photo-capture to bridge to digital work.Require students to predict before checking and explain each step.

Learning Pathway (Concrete → Digital)

Tiles (build meaning) → Matchsticks (see patterns) → Balance Scale (practise the algorithm) → Function Machine (generalise and graph). Each stage reuses the same CT ideas at a higher level of abstraction, so understanding transfers.

Conclusion

In this e-portfolio, I looked at four algebra activities using computational thinking. Each activity helped students break a problem into small steps, find patterns, use letters like x and n to stand for numbers, follow clear steps, explain their reasoning and check their answers.

  • Unplugged activities (Algebra Tiles and Matchstick Patterns) help students understand the idea of balance and patterns by using real objects and working together.
  • Plugged-in activities (Digital Balance Scale and Function Machine) give quick feedback, let students practise more, and show how tables, graphs and rules are connected.
  • Using both together works best. Students first understand the idea with objects, then practise and see it more clearly on the computer.

So, computational thinking helps us plan good learning materials and websites. It guides us on what to break down, what to keep simple, what steps to repeat and how to check learning.

Reflection

What I learned: In every activity, I had to split the problem into small steps. With tiles and matchsticks I could see the algebra. On the computer I could do it faster and get answers checked straight away.

What worked well: Hands-on activities made the ideas easy to understand. The digital activities made practice easier and showed the graph clearly.

What was difficult: Unplugged work takes more time, and students can just click buttons on the computer without thinking about the steps.

How I designed this website: I used simple menus, different colours for unplugged and plugged-in activities, and interactive demos so students can try them. It also works on phones and in dark mode.

What I would improve: I would add a progress tracker, GeoGebra or Desmos tools, and a short quiz at the end of each activity.
